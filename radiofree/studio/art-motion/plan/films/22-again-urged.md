**The one question:** *Who can make the GPS jamming stop?*

**The answer, in one sentence (copy.json `argument`):** Finnish aviation sees GPS interference around the clock. Finland
and the Baltic states took it to the ITU, the UN's radio body, whose board urged Russia to stop, again urged, and then
strongly urged. No reply is on record.

## The motion technique that carries the twist
**PHYS** (paper with weight, `plan/motion-options.md`): every envelope and letter is thrown, falls and lands with a
damped bounce computed as a pure function of the shot's time, so the pile on the mat GROWS in shot 5 as the verbs are
read. The escalation is in the stamps of shot 4: the same desk, the same letter, three stamps, each bigger, the third
in red with a jolt of the whole desk. The first shot's tear is the only glitch in the film, and it shows the effect of
spoofing (the plane is suddenly somewhere it is not), never the method.

## Risks and how the film handles them
- **Attribution.** The film's picture names Russia only as the board's addressee ("urged Russia to stop", TO: THE
  ADMINISTRATION OF THE RUSSIAN FEDERATION), which is the board's own record. Who is jamming is not drawn: no
  transmitter, no Kaliningrad, no flag. The post copy says Finland and Lithuania trace it to Russia, and Moscow denies it.
- **No crash talk.** The plane flies on; nothing falls; no alarms; the pilot's answer is a paper chart.
- **No how-to.** Nothing on screen explains jamming or spoofing.
- **Fair to the ITU.** The joke is not that the board is lazy: it is that a body built on cooperation can only write
  letters. The board's escalation is drawn as earnest (three stamps, each harder), and the tag ends on its next meeting.
- **"On record".** The film says no reply is on record, which is what the ITU's own pages show; it does not say Russia
  never replied anywhere.
- **The 26–30 Oct agenda** is not confirmed, so the last tag says only BOARD MEETS AGAIN.
