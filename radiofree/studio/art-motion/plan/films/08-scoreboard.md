**The one question:** Denmark's bill bans AI copies of your face, unless it's satire. **Who decides what's satire?**
**The answer:** the courts, case by case, and courts have answered both ways since 1831. Two all. Denmark's courts
take the decider.

**Written to the rule** (`PLANNING.md`, 18 Oct): the question first, three facts, the answer last over its image.
Every caption in `papercut/scoreboard/copy.json` has a role and a source.

**CASES IN THIS FILM**, so the tally is honest about being a selection. Copenhagen look, cool and pale (the
Folketinget's grey stone and green copper), so it does not repeat film 5's red and candlelight.

**Facts and fairness:**
- L 49 is a **bill**, not law: the first caption and the tag say so.
- 1831: Philipon was convicted for outrage against the king; the pear was his defence, drawn at that trial. The
  caption says "mocking the king: guilty", not "the pear: guilty".
- Every exhibit mocks someone with power. No parody's content is shown (the Falwell text, the pig drawings are
  out), only the object and the verdict. No politician's face is drawn.
- Left out: Mühl's painting (the 2007 case) and the Moscow puppet. Both are true, but the rule allows three facts,
  and Mühl's own record makes the painting a bad exhibit.
