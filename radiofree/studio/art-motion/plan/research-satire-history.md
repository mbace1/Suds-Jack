# Satire has always worn other people's faces: research for the follow-up to "Satire Excepted"

Compiled 15 Oct 2026 for a papercut follow-up to film 5 (`films/05-satire-excepted.md`).
Every page fetch was blocked in this sandbox (WebFetch DNS failure, proxy 403), so everything
here comes from search excerpts, checked across outlets.

- A quote is marked **VERBATIM** only where an outlet prints it word for word, and it says
  whose wording it is. A translation stays labelled as a translation.
- **SINGLE-SOURCE** marks a claim that rests on one outlet.
- **LEGEND** marks a story that is repeated everywhere but never sourced.

**Thesis for the film:** a court deciding what counts as satire is not new. Since 1831, courts,
censors and ministers have kept being asked whether a joke that wears a real, powerful person's
face is allowed, and they have answered both ways.

**Deliberately excluded:** the 2005 Jyllands-Posten Muhammad cartoons and Charlie Hebdo. They
are religious, they ended in violence, and they are not stories about a real person's likeness
in this sense. Neither is used, alluded to or drawn.

**Hook worth knowing:** *The Great Dictator* premiered in New York on **15 October 1940**, 86
years ago today (moment 6).

## 1 · 1831, Paris: the pear in the dock

- **When / where:** the Paris assize court (cour d'assises), **14 November 1831**.
- **Who:**
  - Charles Philipon, publisher of *La Caricature*.
  - King Louis-Philippe, the face in question.
- **The satire:**
  - Philipon was prosecuted for "outrage against the person of the King" over drawings that
    resembled the king. The case started with an anonymous drawing of a mason plastering over
    the 1830 revolution.
  - His defence was itself a drawing. He sketched **four heads**, the first unmistakably the king
    and the last a **pear**, each looking like the one before. His argument was that resemblance
    cannot be owned: if the first is a crime, so is the pear.
  - He then printed the sequence in *La Caricature* (the "Croquades faites à l'audience du 14
    novembre"), captioned with his own courtroom argument.
- **Contested → outcome:**
  - Philipon was convicted and sentenced to **six months in prison and a 2,000-franc fine**. The
    publisher Aubert and the printer Delaporte were acquitted.
  - The pear then became the king's nickname in print, in songs and on the walls of Paris.
- **Coda, 27 February 1834, and a perfect papercut:**
  - Philipon's daily *Le Charivari* printed its own court verdict (its conviction for a press
    offence) **typeset in the shape of a pear** on its front page.
- **Quote (VERBATIM, in French, Philipon's own printed caption as given by Encyclopædia
  Universalis):** "Et enfin, vous ne sauriez absoudre cette poire, qui ressemble aux croquis
  précédents."
  - In English: "And finally, you could not acquit this pear, which resembles the previous
    sketches."
- **What to draw:**
  - Four card heads flipping from king to pear.
  - The verdict typeset as a pear.
  - Pears chalked across a wall.
- **Sources:**
  - Wikipedia, "Pear (caricature)": https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pear_(caricature)
  - Public Domain Review, "Of Pears and Kings": https://publicdomainreview.org/essay/of-pears-and-kings/
  - CUNY (conviction, fine, six months): https://henripeyrefi.ws.gc.cuny.edu/?p=15690
  - Universalis, Charles Philipon (the French quote): https://www.universalis.fr/encyclopedie/charles-philipon/
  - Sims Reed catalogue (the Croquades supplement): https://books.simsreed.com/books/41815
  - The pear-shaped verdict of 1834: Warwick collection record
    https://olive-panda.lnx.warwick.ac.uk/items/show/126 and Wikipedia, Charles Philipon
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Philipon
  - The pear on the walls: Cabinet magazine https://cabinetmagazine.org/issues/17/archibald.php
- **Confidence: well documented.**
  - One outlier says he was jailed "for a year"; six months is what the court-record summary and
    most sources give.
  - Cabinet describes an *acquittal* after an earlier, May 1831 prosecution. That is a different
    case.
  - "The pear was banned all over France" is **not corroborated**, so it is dropped.
  - The Victor Hugo anecdote of the king tipping a boy who chalked a pear is **unverified**, so it
    is dropped.

## 2 · 1831 to 1832, Paris: Daumier's *Gargantua*

- **When / where:**
  - The lithograph was made for *La Caricature* in **December 1831**, and the stone was seized.
  - The trial was in **February 1832**.
  - Daumier was arrested in **August 1832** and released in **February 1833**.
- **Who:** Honoré Daumier, aged 24, his printer and his print dealer; Louis-Philippe.
- **The satire:**
  - The king is Rabelais's giant, seated on a commode-throne.
  - Ragged taxpayers feed coins up a ramp into his mouth.
  - Below him, well-dressed courtiers catch the favours that fall out.
- **Contested → outcome:**
  - The charges were "incitement to hatred and contempt for the King's government" and
    "offence against the person of the King".
  - Daumier got **six months** and was held at Sainte-Pélagie, then moved to a private clinic.
  - Most sources give a **500-franc** fine; one gives 100 francs.
- **Quote:** none needed. The two charges above are given in quotation marks by Artprice
  (translation).
- **What to draw:** the giant king on his chair, the ramp, and the coins.
- **Sources:**
  - Artprice: https://news.artprice.com/en/14147/gargantua-by-daumier-1831-the-caricature-that-sent-the-artist-to-prison
  - EBSCO Research Starters, Honoré Daumier: https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/honore-daumier
  - Yale News (describes the image): https://news.yale.edu/2018/09/26/yale-art-gallery-exhibit-celebrates-lasting-value-caricature
  - UEN Pressbooks (the 100-franc variant): https://uen.pressbooks.pub/arth2720/chapter/daumier-third-class-carriage/
- **Confidence: documented.** The prison term is consistent; the fine and the time actually
  served vary.

## 3 · 1835, France: the September laws end political caricature

- **When / where:**
  - The press law of **9 September 1835**, passed after the Fieschi attempt on the king's life.
  - *La Caricature*'s last issue was **27 August 1835**. One teaching sheet says 27 September,
    which looks like a typo.
- **Who:** the July Monarchy's government, Philipon, Daumier.
- **What the law did:**
  - No drawing, engraving, lithograph, print or emblem could be published, exhibited or sold
    without the **prior authorisation of the Minister of the Interior** in Paris, or of the
    prefect in the provinces.
  - Offenders went to the correctional courts instead of a jury trial.
- **Outcome:**
  - *La Caricature* closed.
  - Political caricature in France gave way to social satire (Daumier's "mœurs") for years after.
  - *Le Charivari* survived by turning to manners.
- **Quote:** an English translation of the article is quoted by rectoversoblog. Sources number
  the article differently ("Article 18" there; other histories cite art. 20), so **paraphrase it,
  and do not put an article number on screen.**
- **What to draw:** a wall of pears and a ministry rubber stamp ("AUTORISÉ?") coming down on
  each one.
- **Sources:**
  - Daumier Register, *La Caricature* (dates, closure): https://www.daumier.org/reference/newspapers-journals/la-caricature/
  - "Charlie Hebdo's Ancestors", rectoversoblog (the law's text in translation; mentioned here only as a source): https://rectoversoblog.com/?p=1408
  - OpenEdition, *Satire des mœurs et critique sociale ... 1835 à 1848*: https://books.openedition.org/pul/7925
  - arthistoricum, *La Caricature*: https://www.arthistoricum.net/en/subjects/sources/journals-1/satire/carcature
  - EBSCO, Caricature and Censorship: https://ebsco.com/research-starters/communication-and-mass-media/caricature-and-censorship
- **Confidence: well documented.** The article number is disputed; the effect is not.

## 4 · 1871 to 1876, New York and Vigo: Nast's Tweed, and the cartoon as a wanted poster

- **Who:** Thomas Nast of *Harper's Weekly*; William M. "Boss" Tweed of Tammany Hall.
- **The satire:**
  - **"The Brains"** (Harper's Weekly, 21 Oct 1871): Tweed with a **moneybag for a head**.
  - **"Tweed-le-dee and Tilden-dum"** (1 July 1876): Tweed in prison stripes, collaring two boys.
- **Contested → outcome:**
  - **Retaliation.** Tweed had his Board of Education **reject all Harper bids for schoolbooks
    and throw out those already bought**. Princeton puts the destroyed public property at over
    $50,000. Tweed also put a bill before the State Legislature protesting Nast's pictures.
  - **The twist.** After Tweed escaped jail and fled via Cuba, he was arrested at **Vigo, Spain,
    in September 1876**, with the help of a Nast cartoon. Harper's Weekly itself reprinted it on
    7 Oct 1876 as **"The Capture of Tweed — The Picture that Made the Spanish Officials Take Him
    for a 'Child-Stealer'."**
  - In other words, the officials recognised the face and misread the crime.
  - Britannica says the caricature led to his identification and arrest. HISTORY says the
    Spanish police "reportedly" recognised him.
  - He was back in New York's Ludlow Street jail by 23 Nov 1876 and died there in 1878.
- **Quotes:**
  - **VERBATIM**, the Harper's Weekly title above, as catalogued by the Museum of Fine Arts,
    Houston and dilibri.
  - **VERBATIM (Princeton quoting Tweed's protest bill):** "pictures vulgar and blasphemous".
    **SINGLE-SOURCE.**
  - **LEGEND:** "Stop them damned pictures."
    - The wording varies from version to version, and HarpWeek says Tweed "reportedly" said it.
    - Versions trace to Albert Bigelow Paine's 1904 Nast biography, decades later.
    - **Do not put it on screen as a quote.**
  - The **$500,000 bribe** to make Nast "study art in Europe" rests on a family write-up, so it is
    **unverified**.
- **What to draw:**
  - A moneybag head in a top hat.
  - The cartoon pinned up on a quay while officials hold it beside a passenger.
- **Sources:**
  - Princeton Graphic Arts (the textbooks, the bill): https://www.princeton.edu/~graphicarts/2008/09/tweedledee_and_sweedledum.html
  - Britannica, Thomas Nast: https://britannica.com/print/article/404083
  - HISTORY, "Boss Tweed delivered to authorities": https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/november-23/boss-tweed-delivered-to-authorities
  - MFAH record, "The Capture of Tweed": https://emuseum.mfah.org/objects/13454/the-capture-of-tweed--the-picture-that-made-the-spanish-of
  - dilibri, Harper's Weekly 7 Oct 1876, p. 821: https://www.dilibri.de/rlb/content/titleinfo/1802101
  - HISTORY, Nast vs Tweed ("The Brains"): https://www.history.com/articles/thomas-nast-boss-tweed-cartoons
  - HarpWeek (the quote as "reportedly"): https://nastandgreeley.harpweek.com/SubPages/Publishersnote.asp
- **Confidence:**
  - **Well documented:** the schoolbook retaliation and the Vigo capture with the cartoon.
  - **LEGEND:** the famous quote.
  - **Unverified:** the bribe.
  - This is retaliation and identification, **not a court ruling on satire**. Use it as the
    "satire has power, so power hits back" beat.

## 5 · 1934, Copenhagen: Hans Bendix draws Hitler as Pilate (Nordic)

- **Who:**
  - Hans Bendix, cartoonist at *Social-Demokraten* from 1922 to 1950, later at *Politiken*.
  - Adolf Hitler.
  - Hartvig Frisch and Prime Minister Thorvald Stauning, criticised alongside him.
- **The satire:**
  - Bendix's book *Tegning af Tiden* included "**Dollfuss in memoriam**", made after the
    Austrian chancellor's murder in July 1934.
  - It shows **Hitler washing his bloody hands like a modern Pilate**.
- **Contested → outcome:**
  - Frisch's review alerted the German press.
  - Bendix, Frisch and Stauning were sharply criticised in Germany *and* in Denmark, where
    neutrality and trade with Germany were at stake.
  - Bendix co-edited the anti-Nazi journals *Aandehullet* and *Kulturkampen*.
  - During the Second World War he lived **in exile in the USA because of his anti-Nazi
    satirical drawings**.
  - No court case is recorded. Denmark's foreign minister had already warned the editors-in-chief
    about the new regime in March 1933.
- **Quote:** none verbatim; paraphrase only.
- **What to draw:** a basin, a pair of hands and a toothbrush moustache. Keep the Pilate
  reference implicit (the washing of hands), never religious imagery.
- **Sources:**
  - Louise C. Larsen, "Pandens flugt anno 1933. Europas fascister i Hans Bendix' streg",
    *Magasin fra Det Kongelige Bibliotek* 21:3-4 (2008), pp. 57–71: https://tidsskrift.dk/magasin/article/view/66910
  - lex.dk, Hans Bendix (the exile): https://lex.dk/Hans_Bendix
  - Store norske leksikon, Hans Bendix (the exile): https://snl.no/Hans_Bendix
  - The 1933 editors' meeting: tidsskrift.dk, *Magasin*: https://tidsskrift.dk/magasin/article/download/66794/96312/145876
- **Confidence: partly SINGLE-SOURCE.**
  - The drawing and the German reaction come from Larsen 2008 via search excerpts.
  - The exile is in two encyclopaedias.
  - It is good enough for a tag, **not for a whole shot** without reading Larsen first.

## 6 · 1940: *The Great Dictator*, banned

- **When / where:** world premiere **15 October 1940** at the Astor and Capitol theatres in New
  York.
- **Who:** Charles Chaplin as "Adenoid Hynkel" (Hitler); Jack Oakie as "Napaloni" (Mussolini).
- **The satire:**
  - The most famous borrowed face in film: the toothbrush moustache and the salute.
  - The dictator dances alone with an inflatable globe until it pops.
- **Contested → outcome:**
  - **Banned in Germany, Japan, Spain, Peru and Argentina** (Smithsonian).
  - Also **Ireland** and other neutral European states that did not want to offend Hitler
    (Festival de Cannes, IrishCentral).
  - It was effectively unseen across occupied Europe until 1945.
  - **Not found:** a documented wartime ban in Sweden, Finland or Norway (beyond occupation
    itself). Do not claim one. A Swedish 1945 poster says "first showing in Sweden", which is
    unverified as to why.
- **Quote:** none needed.
- **What to draw:** the globe balloon, and BANNED stamps landing on it country by country.
- **Sources:**
  - Smithsonian: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/arts-culture/interview-limited-joins-ranks-these-banned-censored-restricted-movies-180953722/

## 7 · 1987 and 1988: two courts, two answers, one year apart

**7a · Karlsruhe, 3 June 1987: the Strauß caricatures (BVerfGE 75, 369; 1 BvR 313/85)**

- **Who:**
  - Franz Josef Strauß, Minister-President of Bavaria.
  - The cartoonist, named in the judgment only as "Mr H." Rainer Hachfeld is often named, but
    that was **not confirmed** here, so do not name him.
- **The satire:**
  - Caricatures in the magazine *konkret* showed Strauß as a **pig**, including in sexual acts
    with a pig in judicial robes.
- **Outcome:**
  - The Hamburg Higher Regional Court had convicted the cartoonist of insult (§ 185 StGB) on
    17 Jan 1985.
  - The Federal Constitutional Court **dismissed his complaint**: caricatures that attack the core
    of personal honour protected by human dignity (Art. 1(1) Basic Law) fall outside artistic
    freedom.
- **Quote:** the University of Texas English summary (translation; **paraphrase on screen**):
  caricatures "that attack the core of personal honour ... are not covered by the freedom of
  artistic activity".
- **Sources:**
  - Full text, University of Bern: https://www.servat.unibe.ch/dfr/bv075369.html
  - English translation, University of Texas: https://law.utexas.edu/transnational/foreign-law-translations/german/case.php?id=634
- **Draw:** a paper pig in a politician's glasses, and **never** the sexual content.

**7b · Washington, 24 February 1988: *Hustler Magazine v. Falwell*, 485 U.S. 46**

- **Who:** Larry Flynt's *Hustler*; the Rev. Jerry Falwell, televangelist and Moral Majority
  leader.
- **The satire:**
  - A spoof of Campari's "my first time" celebrity ads, with Falwell's face.
  - It carried small print: "ad parody — not to be taken seriously".
- **Outcome:**
  - A jury had awarded Falwell damages for emotional distress.
  - The Supreme Court reversed. Chief Justice Rehnquist wrote for the Court; Justice White
    concurred in the judgment and Justice Kennedy took no part.
  - Public figures cannot recover for a parody that could not reasonably be taken as stating
    actual facts.
  - The opinion sets the case in the tradition of political cartoons, including Nast's attack on
    the Tweed Ring. That comes from law-review coverage; check it against the opinion before
    using it on screen.
- **Quotes (VERBATIM, opinion of the Court):**
  - "'Outrageousness' in the area of political and social discourse has an inherent
    subjectiveness about it which would allow a jury to impose liability on the basis of the
    jurors' tastes or views, or perhaps on the basis of their dislike of a particular
    expression."
  - "The art of the cartoonist is often not reasoned or evenhanded, but slashing and one-sided."
- **Sources:**
  - Cornell LII: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/485/46
  - law.resource.org: https://law.resource.org/pub/us/case/reporter/US/485/485.US.46.86-1278.html
  - Pepperdine (the vote): https://publicpolicy.pepperdine.edu/academics/research/faculty-research/constitutional-law-rehnquist/1980s/hus_v_fal.htm
  - FIRE: https://www.fire.org/supreme-court/hustler-magazine-v-falwell
  - Law Weekly (the Nast comparison): https://www.lawweekly.org/col/2019/10/2/comic-relief-the-supreme-court-decision-that-saved-political-satire
- **Draw:** a liquor-ad poster with a face and the small print. **Never** the parody's content.

**Confidence: well documented (both).** On screen this is the cleanest "courts answer both ways"
beat: a year apart, two high courts, opposite answers.

## 8 · 1995 and 2000, Moscow: *Kukly*, the puppets

- **Who:**
  - NTV's *Kukly* (Puppets), a Spitting Image-style rubber-puppet show.
  - Writer Viktor Shenderovich.
  - Boris Yeltsin; Vladimir Putin.
- **Contested → outcome, 1995:**
  - In July 1995 the Prosecutor General opened criminal proceedings over an episode showing
    **Yeltsin's puppet as a homeless drunk**. The charge was "conscious and public humiliation"
    of officials' honour and dignity, under Article 131, punishable by up to two years of
    "corrective labour".
  - The charges were later dropped and the prosecutor was fired. The show flourished.
- **Contested → outcome, 2000:**
  - In February 2000, Putin's puppet played Hoffmann's *Little Zaches*, and his campaign
    associates published a letter urging prosecution.
  - In late May 2000 the anchor announced on air that the **Putin puppet would not appear**,
    calling it an experiment prompted by government pressure.
  - Shenderovich says an unnamed top official asked for it.
  - In the next episode, "Ten Commandments", Putin's puppet was replaced by **a cloud over a
    mountain top and a burning bush**.
  - NTV was taken over in 2001, and the show ended in 2002 (some sources say 2003).
- **Quote:** **VERBATIM (CSMonitor, quoting the prosecutor's charge):** "conscious and public
  humiliation of [government officials'] honor and dignity".
- **What to draw:** a puppet on its hook; then an empty hook with a paper cloud drifting into
  frame. Skip the bush.
- **Context, UK:**
  - *Spitting Image* (ITV, 1984–96) is the format's origin.
  - Its targets complained by letter: Cambridge University Library holds the complaint letters,
    including David Steel's about his tiny puppet in David Owen's pocket.
  - **No lawsuit by a politician was found** in this research, so it is a complaint and not a
    case.
- **Sources:**
  - Christian Science Monitor, 19 July 1995: https://www.csmonitor.com/1995/0719/19062.html
  - VOA via GlobalSecurity, 30 May 2000: https://www.globalsecurity.org/wmd/library/news/russia/2000/000530-rus1.htm
  - The Globe and Mail, "Piqued Putin cancels satirical TV show": https://www.theglobeandmail.com/news/world/piqued-putin-cancels-satirical-tv-show/article1040114
  - Persuasion, "25 Years Ago, Russia Had Its Own Kimmel Moment": https://www.persuasion.community/p/25-years-ago-russia-had-its-own-kimmel
  - Wikipedia, Puppets (TV series) (the cloud episode): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puppets_(TV_series)
  - Spitting Image letters: https://www.cam.ac.uk/stories/spitting-image-exhibition and
    https://markpack.org.uk/48622/did-spitting-image-damage-david-steel
- **Confidence: well documented.**
  - The 1995 case and the 2000 removal both have contemporary reports.
  - That the Kremlin asked is Shenderovich's claim, so present it as *his*.

## 9 · 1998 to 2007, Vienna and Strasbourg: photographs of real heads pasted on painted bodies

- **Who:**
  - Otto Mühl's painting *Apocalypse*, 450 × 360 cm, in the Vienna Secession's centenary show of
    1998.
  - 34 public figures, including Jörg Haider and **Walter Meischberger**, a former FPÖ
    general secretary and MP.
  - The association Vereinigung Bildender Künstler.
- **The satire:**
  - The bodies were **painted** in sexual scenes.
  - The **heads were blown-up newspaper photographs**: a real person's actual face, cut out and
    pasted onto a body that was not theirs. It was an analogue deepfake.
- **Contested → outcome:**
  - On 12 June 1998 a visitor covered Meischberger's section in **red paint**. Der Standard names
    the vandal as Martin Humer.
  - Meischberger sued. Vienna's Commercial Court dismissed the case in 1999, but on appeal the
    painting was placed under an **injunction unlimited in time and space** in 2000.
  - On **25 January 2007** the European Court of Human Rights (4–3) found a violation of
    Article 10. The painting was a **caricature using satirical elements**, the injunction was
    disproportionate, and Austria paid costs and damages.
- **Quote:** paraphrase only; the Court called the painting satire that contributed to public
  debate.
- **What to draw:** a big canvas with paper photo-heads on painted bodies (keep the bodies
  abstract), one head splashed red, and a Strasbourg gavel.
- **Sources:**
  - Columbia Global Freedom of Expression: https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/cases/vereinigung-bildender-kunstler-v-austria/
  - ECtHR press release, 25 Jan 2007: https://globalfreedomofexpression.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Press-Release-Vereinigung-Bildender-Künstler-v.-Austria-25.01.2007.pdf
  - The judgment: https://ferenda.lagen.nu/dom/echr/001-79213
  - Der Standard (the vandal): https://derstandard.at/1369361719976/Aktionskuenstler-Otto-Muehl-87-jaehrig-gestorben
- **Confidence: well documented.** The figure count is 34 in the judgment and 33 in Der Standard.
- **Note for the owner:**
  - Mühl was convicted in 1991 of sexual offences against minors at his Friedrichshof commune and
    sentenced to seven years.
  - A 2026 retrospective in Vienna drew protests.
  - So the film should show the *painting and the court*, never celebrate the artist. Consider
    skipping this one for that reason alone.
  - Sources: ORF https://wien.orf.at/stories/3370329/ and Austria-Forum
    https://austria-forum.org/af/AustriaWiki/Otto_Muehl

## 10 · 2023 to 2024, Berlin: the fake chancellor

- **Who:**
  - The art collective Zentrum für Politische Schönheit (ZPS).
  - Chancellor Olaf Scholz.
  - The German federal government, as the plaintiff.

## Landing: the law catches up (2026)

- **EU AI Act, Article 50(4)** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689), applies from **2 Aug 2026**.
  - Deployers must disclose deepfakes.
  - Where the content is part of an "**evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or
    analogous work or programme**", the duty shrinks to disclosing it "in an appropriate manner
    that does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work" (**VERBATIM**, AI Act Service
    Desk).
  - The Commission's draft guidelines (May 2026) say the exception holds only where the
    character of the work is clear from format and context. One source says the final guidelines
    came on 20 July 2026 (**SINGLE-SOURCE**).
  - Sources:
    - https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-50
    - https://www.srd-rechtsanwaelte.de/en/blog/labelling-requirements-ai-act-deepfakes
    - https://www.globalpolicywatch.com/2026/05/10-takeaways-european-commission-draft-guidelines-on-ai-transparency-under-the-eu-ai-act/
    - https://www.mccannfitzgerald.com/knowledge/data-privacy-and-cyber-risk/ai-transparency-european-commissions-guidelines-on-article-50-part-2-deployer-obligations
- **Denmark, bill L 49**, tabled **8 Oct 2026** by Culture Minister Zenia Stampe.
  - It amends the Copyright Act with a new §73a: no realistic digital imitation of a person's
    appearance, voice or movements without consent.
  - The publisher has to prove consent.
  - Protection lasts 50 years after death.
  - The target date is 1 Jan 2027.
  - **Satire, parody and political or social criticism stay allowed** unless the fake is
    misinformation that seriously endangers others.
  - The minister concedes the line is hard to draw. **The courts decide case by case.**
  - It follows a June 2025 political agreement under the previous minister, Jakob Engel-Schmidt.
  - Sources:
    - Euronews: https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/08/denmark-introduces-a-bill-protecting-peoples-faces-and-voices-from-deepfakes
    - PPC Land: https://ppc.land/denmarks-deepfake-bill-makes-publishers-prove-consent-from-january-2027/
    - sn.dk: https://www.sn.dk/art6657358/koebenhavn-kommune/politik/kulturminister-vil-forbyde-at-dele-ai-kopier-af-personer
  - **It is a bill, not law**, so say so on screen, as film 5 did.

## Two angles for the 6-shot follow-up

**The format:**
- Papercut, 6 shots of about 6.25 s each, 37.5 s in all, then the station sign-off.
- Captions are 8 words or fewer per shot (word counts in brackets).
- Real names only for what verifiably happened.
- The joke stays on power and on the law. **Never** on people harmed by deepfakes, and never
  reproduce a parody's offensive content (the Falwell text, the Strauß drawings, DF's Eid line).

### Angle A · "The Pear's Long Walk": faces on trial since 1831

**Insight:**
- Every era invented a new way to wear a powerful person's face: a pencil, a rubber puppet, a
  voice clone.
- Every era then asked someone official whether that was allowed. Denmark is just the latest to
  ask.
- **The device:** one paper pear walks through every shot as a Hitchcock-cameo (on a wall, a quay,
  a cinema seat), and in shot 6 it hangs on the © mask rail from film 5.

### Angle B · "The Scoreboard": Courts v. Satire, 1831–2027

- People talk about this as if Denmark were opening a new question. It is a fixture that has been

- **The device:** an old flip-card scoreboard (COURTS | SATIRE) over a paper courtroom bench.
- Each shot puts an exhibit on the bench and a card flips.
- The board is labelled small, **"CASES IN THIS FILM"**, so the tally is honest about being a
  selection.

### Which to make

- **B is the stronger film**:
  - one device;
  - an honest, checkable tally;
  - a payoff that is a joke about the institution and not about anyone's face;
  - every shot after 1876 is an actual ruling.
- **A is the prettier film** (the pear's walk) and the more Nordic one, with Copenhagen in shot 5.
- **Both share the same opening**, the 1831 courtroom, and Philipon's own argument that a face
  cannot be owned is the whole film in one drawing.
