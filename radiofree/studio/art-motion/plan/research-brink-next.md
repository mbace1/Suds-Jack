## 1. Tailgated (a Russian satellite parks beside a Finnish company's radar satellite)

**What happened.**
- **The satellite:** ICEYE-X36 is a radar-imaging satellite from ICEYE, a Finnish-founded company. It is registered
  to the company's US subsidiary, and its images have been used by Ukraine's military intelligence.
- **Late September:** Kosmos 2612, a Russian military satellite launched in spring 2026, began a series of engine
  burns that matched X36's orbit (Satnews).
- **15–16 Oct:** per the tracking firm LeoLabs, it closed from about **350 km to under 5 km** by early on 16 Oct, then
  kept station nearby. Astronomer Jonathan McDowell said it was stationkeeping "within 5 km or less".
- **Closest pass:** estimates conflict, from about 500 m (LeoLabs, "uncertain") to a few hundred metres (Aerotime)
  to 26 m (TWZ; Militarnyi) and 85 feet (Washington Times). Public orbital data cannot settle it.
- **Mon 19 Oct:** LeoLabs reported Kosmos 2612 moving away. Nothing collided; no damage or disruption was reported.
- **First of its kind?** McDowell said it may be the first rendezvous in low Earth orbit between two active
  satellites where one owner did not give approval.
- **The rules:** a space-law professor told Axios there is **no strict legal limit** on how close satellites may get,
  though operators generally keep **about six miles (~10 km)** apart. Scientific American: few official international
  regulations cover such manoeuvres. The 1967 Outer Space Treaty asks for "due regard" and consultation, with no
  distances.
- **Responses:** Russia has said nothing. ICEYE declined to comment. The US Defense Department declined to comment;
  the US Space Force has called out earlier manoeuvres (Scientific American).
- **Background:** in May 2026, four Russian satellites (Kosmos 2610–2613) came within about 13 km of X36 (Spiegel,
  citing Okapi:Orbit).

## 2. Break Glass in Emergency (a border law never used, extended unchanged to 2028)

**What happened.**
- **The crossings:** Finland's land crossings to Russia have been closed since December 2023. On 4 Jun 2026 the
  government decided to keep them closed, assessing the threat of "instrumentalised migration" as high but stable.
- **The law:** the Act on Temporary Measures to Combat Instrumentalised Migration (the "Border Security Act") took
  effect on **22 Jul 2024 for one year**. In a restricted area and as a last resort, it lets the government limit the
  receiving of asylum applications at the border. It was passed as an exception to the Constitution.
- **First extension:** on **4 Jun 2025** Parliament extended it to **31 Dec 2026**, by **168–29** (urgency needs
  five-sixths, then two-thirds to pass).
- **Never applied:** the government's own situation page says the Act has **not been applied**. Using it would need a
  separate decision.
- **3 Sep 2026:** the government decided to propose a second extension, to **31 Dec 2028**, with **no changes to the
  content**.
- **The Chancellor of Justice**, in the consultation, called the government's reasons **thin** ("niukat"). The
  Chancellor said necessity should now be judged against the law's total length and the fact that its powers have
  **never once** been used, while not disputing the authorities' threat assessment.
- **16 Oct:** the Constitutional Law Committee found the extension can be enacted as a limited exception to the
  Constitution and is necessary, and said the government must watch the security and legal situation closely. Two
  members (Greens and Left Alliance) dissented.
- **Next:** the plenary vote, expected in the coming weeks. The law otherwise lapses on 31 Dec 2026.

## 3. Again Urged (the UN radio referee and Baltic GPS jamming)

**What happened.**
- **In Finland:** Traficom reported in **April 2026** that GNSS (GPS) interference is seen in aviation **around the
  clock in southern and central Finland**. Observations rose clearly from February and peaked in March; spoofing (false
  positions) was also detected. Traficom links it to Russia's "self-protection" jamming and in April filed new official
  interference reports **with Russia and with the ITU**.
- **In Lithuania:** the regulator RRT told Reuters (26 May) that Russia's GPS-spoofing antennas in Kaliningrad grew
  from **3 in early 2025 to 36**, with an estimated reach of about 450 km, covering Finland and the Baltic states.
  Russia denies it and blames Western smear tactics.
- **The referee:** the ITU (the UN agency for radio) has a Radio Regulations Board that rules on harmful interference.
  Meeting after meeting it has instructed its staff to **urge** Russia to stop interference to satellite-navigation
  receivers in Estonia, Finland, Latvia and Lithuania. At its 100th meeting (Nov 2025) it decided to **"again urge"**.
  Its 102nd meeting record says **no response had been received** from Russia.
- **Next:** the board's next meeting is **26–30 Oct 2026** in Geneva. The ITU's top conference (PP-26, Doha, 9–27 Nov)
  then elects the board's members.
- **Escalation on paper:** in June 2025, 13 EU states including Finland wrote to the Commission. The Council's
  secretariat then floated suspending Russia's and Belarus's right to register radio use at the ITU while the
  interference continues. No sign it was adopted.
- **Context, not for the film:** on 18 Oct a stray drone forced a one-hour shelter alert in Lithuania. Ukraine
  apologised and said Russian electronic warfare may have diverted it (Reuters). Its origin is under investigation.

## 4. The Owners' Responsibility (three hits on satellite-internet ground stations)

**What happened.**
- **Poland, ~23 Sep:** a fire at a Starlink ground station in Wola Krobowska, about 40 km south of Warsaw. Prosecutors
  opened a sabotage case and said there were reasonable grounds to suspect Russian services ordered it.
- **12–13 Oct:** two men were detained in Germany at Poland's request, on charges of sabotage on behalf of a foreign
  intelligence service. Poland's special-services minister said there was "no doubt" Russian intelligence was behind
  it; the prime minister called it hybrid warfare. On 16 Oct the Internal Security Agency (ABW) published its findings.
  Poland has named no handler in public.
- **Bulgaria, reported 12–14 Oct:** 11 of 17 Starlink antennas at a site near Sofia were set on fire, first reported by
  an opposition MP. The prime minister confirmed it on 14 Oct. The interior minister told parliament that **responsibility
  for the security of such sites lies with their owners**, while police had added protection at similar sites. Bulgaria
  has not confirmed any Russian involvement.
- **Lithuania, 18 Oct ~02:00:** an attempted arson at a Starlink station on the outskirts of Vilnius, inside a
  data-centre operator's facility that leases space to SpaceX. It was put out quickly, with minor damage and no
  disruption. Officials called it sabotage and linked it to Russia; Moscow called the accusations baseless. No suspects
  had been detained as of 19 Oct.
- **Why these sites:** reports say the stations help carry internet traffic used in Ukraine (Spiegel on Poland; Reuters
  on Lithuania's view that it was aimed at Ukraine).
- **Finland:** Supo's head of counter-intelligence told Yle on 28 Sep that the sabotage threat in Europe has grown, that
  in Finland the defence industry and support for Ukraine are the most exposed, and that there is no sign of immediate
  danger: be prepared, not afraid.

## 5. Open, Sealed (Finland's bill to keep defence inventions secret)

**What happened.**
- **15 Oct:** the government sent Parliament a bill for a new act **on protecting the secrecy of defence inventions**,
  replacing the **1967** act.
- **Old rule:** under the 1967 act, the state could **expropriate** (compulsorily buy) a defence invention after a
  patent application.
- **New rule:** the state could order the invention kept **secret without taking it**. The holder keeps ownership but
  loses free use. Each secrecy decision lasts **at most one year at a time**, and the holder is to be **fully
  compensated** for likely losses. It covers inventions mainly tied to products under national export control.
- **In the draft:** a secret invention is **not granted a patent**; the application is sealed and waits. Inventors
  would also have to file their **first application in Finland**.
- **Why:** the minister said it would help Finnish companies take part in NATO projects, while restricting inventors'
  rights.
- **The watchdog:** on 25 Sep the Council of Regulatory Impact Analysis said the effects on businesses had been
  assessed **only at a general level**, and asked how a secrecy order's costs differ from expropriation. It also noted
  that the current rules discourage patenting defence inventions at all.
- **Timing:** intended to take effect **1 Jun 2027**, alongside the new Patents Act passed in May 2026.

**Sources.**

  inventions act", 15 Oct 2026: https://valtioneuvosto.fi/en/-/1410877/government-proposes-new-defence-inventions-act ·

**Quotes.** None needed.

**The one question:** *Who decides your invention is a state secret?*

**Three facts:**
1. "Patent" comes from the Latin for "open": you publish the idea, and the law protects it.
2. Finland's 1967 law let the state take a defence invention. A bill sent to Parliament on 15 October would instead
   keep it secret, a year at a time, and you keep it.
3. The government's own impact watchdog said the cost to business was assessed "only at a general level".

**Punchline (≤ 12 words):**
- Main: *"'Patent' means 'open'. This one comes sealed."*
- Alternative: *"You still own your invention. You just can't mention it."*

**Risks.**
- **It is a bill.** Say "would" and "a bill". It has not passed.
- **Secrecy itself is not the joke.** Defence secrecy is legitimate, and the bill is milder than the 1967 rule it
