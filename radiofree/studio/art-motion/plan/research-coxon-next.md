## 1. Per Caller (the FCC may let AI voices call your cellphone)

**What happened.**
- **Feb 2024 (background):** the FCC ruled that AI-generated voices count as "artificial" voices under the
  Telephone Consumer Protection Act. Robocalls to cellphones using them need the recipient's prior consent.
- **31 Aug 2026:** the Club for Growth, a conservative political group, petitioned the FCC for a waiver. Political
  calls to cellphones could then use AI-generated or prerecorded voices **without prior consent**.
- **What it offers in return:** no more than **three calls per 30 days per caller** to each person. Opt-outs would be
  processed within **10 days**. The petition also asks for a broader exemption, not only a waiver for itself.
- **4 Sep:** the FCC's Consumer and Governmental Affairs Bureau opened comment in CG Docket 02-278 (Public Notice
  DA 26-940). Comments were due **5 Oct**, and replies were due **19 Oct (today)**.
- **Timing:** reports say the Republican-led FCC could rule **as soon as late October**, before Election Day on
  **3 Nov**. The public notice itself sets no decision date.
- **Opposition:** Commissioner Anna Gomez, the lone Democrat, opposes it. The National Consumer Law Center says the
  limit is weak, because every campaign, PAC and non-profit counts as a separate caller.
- **The group's case:** political speech should be treated like other wireless calls, such as delivery and
  appointment reminders. It argues that calls to landlines are already allowed and that the change would help reach
  voters, including in more languages.

## 2. That's a Wednesday (the chatbot gave the wrong Election Day)

**What happened.**
- **5 Oct, NPR:** "People are asking ChatGPT to help them decide how to vote in the midterms." Voters described using
  chatbots for down-ballot races and to compare candidates. NPR cites Pew: about half of US adults use AI chatbots.
- **The survey:** a September survey by the American Association of Political Consultants Foundation found about **one
  voter in five** consults AI chatbots for election news and voting guidance, at similar rates in both parties.
- **3 Sep, the ISD study:** the Institute for Strategic Dialogue published "Chatbots and the Ballot Box". It tested six
  chatbots across ten states with 2,400 prompts. **29%** of answers to basic voting questions were incomplete,
  inaccurate or outdated. Spanish answers were significantly worse across every model; one outlet gives **71% accuracy
  in English against 55% in Spanish**.
- **The date error:** in the ISD tests, one chatbot (Meta's Muse Spark) misidentified Election Day **twice**, giving
  **4 November 2026**. Election Day is **Tuesday 3 November**, so 4 November is a Wednesday.
- **5 Oct, California:** CITED (the California Initiative for Technology and Democracy, a Common Cause project) released
  its own test. Anthropic's Claude wrongly said Los Angeles mayoral candidate Nithya Raman had lost the June primary;
  she advanced to the November runoff. In two cases ChatGPT asked whether users meant "a Michigan/local proposal" when
  they asked about California's Propositions 4 and 5.
- **Linking to official sources:** a separate study reported by CyberScoop found chatbots linked to a state election
  website less than 40% of the time.

## 3. Label Sold Separately (most AI-made political ads don't say so)

**What happened.**
- **16 Oct, New York Times:** "$137 Million in A.I.-Generated Ads Flood the Midterms". Citing the Wesleyan Media Project
  (and, per one summary, AdImpact), campaigns and political groups have spent **more than $137 million** airing **about
  220** ads made at least partly with AI, across 34 states.
- **The jump:** Wesleyan's co-director Erika Franklin Fowler said the project found **less than $50,000** of AI ad
  spending in the whole 2024 cycle.
- **Labels:** only **27%** of the ads carried a disclaimer saying they contained AI-generated content, per Gizmodo. The
  project's 4 Sep update had found 69% of AI ads, and 78% of the spending, unlabelled.
- **Partisan split:** Republican candidates and groups account for **81%** of the ads and **88%** of the spending.
- **Deepfakes:** nearly half the AI ads in the September count depicted real politicians.
- **The law:** about 30–31 states require AI disclosure in political ads. There is no federal rule. NCSL, per Axios,
  counts 31 states regulating political deepfakes, at least 28 of which require disclosure.
- **One example from the NYT:** an unlabelled ad from a New York governor's campaign showing AI likenesses of Mayor
  Mamdani and Governor Hochul promoting a fictional pill. Not needed for the film.

## 4. The Building Is on the Ballot (neighbours vote on AI's data centres)

**What happened.**
- **The count:** Ballotpedia (21 Sep) counts **38 local data-centre measures** in **eight states** in 2026, with **34 on
  the 3 Nov ballot**. Ohio has about 20 of them, across 17 jurisdictions. S&P Global (1 Oct) and political.org (8 Oct)
  repeat the 34. Other tallies range from 28 localities (Multistate) to 39 (Newsweek).
- **How they got there:** 82% (31 of 38) were put on the ballot by **citizen petition**. Seven were referred by local
  councils.
- **What they do:** some ban large data centres outright (Alhambra, CA; Hubbard, OH), some set power thresholds (Urbana,
  OH: over 7.5 MW; Conneaut, OH: over 25 MW), some require a future public vote, and nine are advisory.
- **Public opinion:** a UMass Amherst national poll (released about 14 Sep, 1,000 adults) found **11%** would support an
  AI data centre in their own community and **65%** would oppose it. Republicans opposed it about 3 to 1. Gallup (May)
  found about 7 in 10 opposed.
- **Campaign spending (context):** NPR/AdImpact (5 Sep) counted more than **$45M** of ads mentioning data centres in
  this year's races, from both parties.
- **Bridge to *Paid For By*:** that film's NYT source found none of the AI super PACs' 95 ads mentioned data centres.

## 5. The Typos Were the Security (AI-written scams aimed at election offices)

**What happened.**
- **Fri 16 Oct:** the FBI and CISA issued a joint public service announcement (IC3, **I-101626-PSA**). It warns that
  criminals are using AI to make phishing emails and texts more convincing, and it names election officials, candidates
  and campaigns as attractive targets.
- **The aim:** to steal passwords or other sensitive data in an effort to disrupt the midterms. It also mentions
  AI-generated audio and video used to impersonate real people.
- **The advice:** use phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication. Verify unusual requests through a separate
  channel, rather than judging by whether the message reads naturally.
- **No breach:** the alert reports no confirmed breach of 2026 election systems.
- **Background, the support that shrank:**
  - **March 2025:** CISA ended its funding (about $10M a year) for the cooperative agreement that ran the Election
    Infrastructure ISAC, the shared threat-alert network for election offices. It said the work "no longer effectuates
    department priorities". EI-ISAC now runs on paid membership with reduced reach (Democratic senators' letter, 2 Sep
    2026).
  - CISA also closed its small election-security office (AP).
  - Headcount fell from about 3,300 (Jan 2025) to 2,389 (Nextgov, May 2026).
  - CISA says it remains ready to help protect election infrastructure.
